Glass Court Squash

A real squash court is 6.4m wide and 9.75m deep, seen here from above. The strip at the top is the front wall, viewed head-on — every shot leaves a mark on it, so you can see why a rally ended.

How squash works

Two players share one enclosed court and hit the same ball alternately. Every shot must reach the front wall. You may let the ball bounce on the floor once before you hit it — or volley it before it bounces at all.

The three ways to lose a rally

  • The tin. A 48cm board across the bottom of the front wall. Hit it and the rally is over — it's the equivalent of the net in tennis.
  • Out. Above the red line. It's 4.57m high on the front wall, sloping down the side walls to 2.13m at the back.
  • Two bounces. Fail to reach the ball before its second bounce.

Serving

  • One foot in the service box.
  • The serve must hit the front wall above the service line (1.78m) and below the out line.
  • It must then land in the opposite back quarter.
  • Win the rally and you serve again, from the other box.

Scoring

Point-a-rally to 11, and you must win by two. Matches are best of five games. Whoever wins the rally serves next, whether they served it or not.

The ball decides the difficulty

Squash balls are graded by a coloured dot, and the grading is exactly how fast and how lively they are. Clubs hand a blue ball to beginners because it bounces high and moves slowly, giving you time to get there. The double yellow is the competition ball: quick, and so dead it drops out of the air rather than bouncing up to you. Pick one under the court — it changes every mode, including practice and two-player.

Tactics, in one line

Hit it long and tight to the side wall, then get back to the T — the marked spot in the middle. Whoever holds the T does less running.

One rule this game leaves out: let and stroke. In real squash both players occupy the same space, so most disputes are about interference — did your opponent block your swing, did they clear out of the way. Judging that needs body position, swing arc and a referee's opinion; it's the most argued-about part of the sport, and professional referees regularly disagree. Rather than fake it, players here simply pass through each other. Everything else above is enforced exactly as written.
